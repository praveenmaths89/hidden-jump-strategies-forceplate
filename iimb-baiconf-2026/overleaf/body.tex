\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Force plates are now standard equipment in many high-performance sports programmes, and the countermovement jump (CMJ) is the test most often performed on them. The test takes a few seconds, causes little fatigue and responds to training and to neuromuscular fatigue \parencite{claudino2017,gathercole2015}. The vendor software identifies the phases of each jump and reports many variables per trial, such as jump height, peak forces, impulses and phase durations \parencite{mcmahon2018}. Few practitioners use all of them. Guidance recommends picking a handful that suit the purpose of testing, whether performance profiling, fatigue monitoring or rehabilitation \parencite{bishop2023}. We call these vendor-computed values vendor metrics. When two athletes post similar vendor metrics, their jumps are usually treated as equivalent.

That practice rests on a premise that is rarely tested. Many organisations reduce a rich measurement record to a few summary indicators, and for any of them the question is whether those indicators keep the information their decisions need. Motor-control research gives reason for doubt. A movement goal can be reached in more than one way, and trial-to-trial variation in execution is often functional rather than error \parencite{davids2003,bartlett2007,preatoni2013}. Two jumps of the same height may therefore follow different force--time paths, on the way up and on the way down.

We test that possibility in routine force-plate records of male football players in India, representing each jump by its full take-off and landing force--time curve. The analysis is organised around three questions. (1) What are the main modes of variation of these curves, and what do they mean mechanically? (2) Do trials with matched jump height, or matched relative concentric peak force, have more similar curves than the sample as a whole? (3) What share of each mode do four vendor metrics, age and body mass explain once repeated trials per athlete are allowed for? Two further analyses split the curve variation into between-athlete, between-session and within-session parts, and repeat the work with a second definition of the landing phase.

The methods are standard. What is new is the use made of them. Matching trials on a metric shows directly how much curve shape that metric leaves open. The landing, which every trial records but most CMJ studies set aside, is analysed together with take-off. And explained variance is estimated with the athlete, not the trial, as the independent unit. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews prior work, Section~\ref{sec:methods} sets out the data and methods, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} to \ref{sec:conclusion} discuss them, draw out implications for practice and conclude.

\section{Literature review}
\label{sec:lit}

The three questions draw on two bodies of work, one built on discrete CMJ variables and the other on whole waveforms. Within the first, no single kinetic or temporal variable has been found to account for vertical-jump performance on its own \parencite{dowling1993}. Better and poorer jumpers distribute force differently across unweighting, braking and propulsion \parencite{sole2018}, and force--time profiles differ by sex and sport \parencite{laffaye2014}. How force develops within each sub-phase is linked to the height achieved \parencite{harry2018}. More recent work separates outcome measures, such as jump height, from variables that describe how the jump was executed, such as countermovement depth and phase timing \parencite{harry2024}. The second kind of variable can reveal fatigue that jump height misses \parencite{gathercole2015}. Curve-level analyses of force, velocity and power have also shown training effects spread across the movement rather than at isolated points \parencite{cormie2009}.

Waveform methods avoid choosing variables in advance. Principal component analysis (PCA) of time-normalised curves, and functional PCA, express each curve as a mean plus a weighted sum of a few uncorrelated shape patterns, with one weight (score) per trial and pattern \parencite{jolliffe2002,ramsay2005,jolliffe2016}. Biomechanists have used these tools for gait in knee osteoarthritis \parencite{deluzio2007}, knee motion during jumping \parencite{ryan2006}, running in Achilles tendinopathy \parencite{donoghue2008} and rowing \parencite{warmenhoven2019}. \textcite{brandon2013} set out ways to read the resulting patterns. In jump research, waveform methods described performance-related features better than discrete points \parencite{richter2014b}, detected gains in jump performance \parencite{floria2019}, and extracted more information once curves were aligned on movement events \parencite{richter2014a,moudy2018}.

This literature leaves two gaps. First, almost all of it stops at take-off. The same plates record the landing in the same trial, yet it is usually set aside, although there are reasons to look at it. \textcite{leppanen2017} reported a higher risk of anterior cruciate ligament injury among young female basketball and floorball players who landed stiffly with high vertical force. Jump and landing measures show some value for injury prediction \parencite{pedley2020}. CMJ landings also change with the effort an athlete believes they are giving \parencite{hite2025}. Second, waveform studies show that curves contain more than discrete variables do, but they seldom measure how much curve shape the routinely reported metrics miss. We are not aware of a study that asks whether jumps matched on such a metric also have matched curves. Large routine datasets from youth football, collected under field conditions, make the question answerable.

\section{Data and methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

The dataset used to answer these questions was collected for monitoring, not for research, and several of the choices below follow from that. We describe its source and protocol first, then the construction of the curves and finally the statistical analysis.

\subsection{Setting, participants and protocol}

The design was retrospective and cross-sectional. The data were CMJ trials recorded during routine testing of male football players at a private sports physiotherapy and performance centre in India between \testPeriod. The centre shared the data with the authors under a signed non-disclosure and data-sharing agreement. All CMJ trials of male football players held in the centre's account were downloaded through the vendor's application programming interface: \nTrialsAll{} trials from \nAthAll{} athletes in \nSessAll{} sessions. A session is one test occasion, at which an athlete performed one or more trials. Athlete, session and trial identifiers were replaced by pseudonymous codes before analysis. The analysis files hold no names, team names, birth dates, test dates or vendor identifiers. The first author keeps the code key separately. Age at test, to 0.1 year, was derived before the identifiers were removed. At their first analysed test, \nMinors{} of the \nAth{} analysed athletes (\pctMinors\%) were younger than 18 years. \ethics{The study was approved by [Ethics committee name, approval number and date to be inserted]. [Written informed consent was obtained from adult athletes, and written consent from a parent or guardian, together with the athlete's assent, was obtained for athletes under 18 years.] / [The committee waived the requirement for consent for the secondary use of de-identified routine monitoring data.] [Delete the option that does not apply.] The study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki.}

Trials were recorded on VALD ForceDecks dual force plates (VALD Performance, Brisbane, Australia). Each plate measures the vertical ground reaction force under one foot, and the two signals were summed. The sampling rate was 1,000\,Hz for most trials and 500\,Hz for \nFsLowAnalysed{} of the \nTrials{} analysed trials (\nFsLow{} of the \nTrialsAll{} extracted). Because every phase was later resampled to the same grid, this difference is unlikely to matter. Sessions contained a median of \trialsPerSessMedian{} trials, and athletes attended a median of \sessPerAthMedian{} session (maximum \sessPerAthMax). Centre staff ran the tests as part of routine assessment, not under a research protocol. Warm-up, verbal instructions and rest intervals were therefore not recorded with the data, and no landing technique was prescribed. For each trial the vendor software supplied the four vendor metrics used here: jump height (from flight time), concentric peak force, eccentric braking impulse and concentric impulse in the first 100\,ms of the concentric phase. Contraction time, flight time, countermovement depth and peak landing force were also exported, for interpretation only.

\subsection{Quality control and curve construction}
\label{sec:curves}

Three events came from the vendor output. Movement onset $t_{\mathrm{on}}$ was taken directly. Take-off was $t_{\mathrm{to}} = t_{\mathrm{on}} + T_{\mathrm{c}}$, with $T_{\mathrm{c}}$ the contraction time, and touch-down was $t_{\mathrm{td}} = t_{\mathrm{to}} + T_{\mathrm{f}}$, with $T_{\mathrm{f}}$ the flight time \parencite{owen2014,mcmahon2018,harry2020}. The take-off phase spans $t_{\mathrm{on}}$ to $t_{\mathrm{to}}$. Two landing definitions were used, because the literature offers no agreed end point for the CMJ landing. In the main analysis, the landing ends at the lowest point of the centre of mass (COM). At that instant the downward velocity gained during the fall has been reduced to zero, so the impact has been absorbed. COM velocity after touch-down was obtained from the impulse--momentum relationship \parencite{linthorne2001,street2001}:
\begin{equation}
v(t) = -\frac{g\,T_{\mathrm{f}}}{2} + \int_{t_{\mathrm{td}}}^{t} \frac{F(s) - m g}{m}\,\mathrm{d}s,
\qquad
t_{\mathrm{lc}} = \min\{\, t > t_{\mathrm{td}} : v(t) \ge 0 \,\}.
\label{eq:velocity}
\end{equation}
Here $F$ is total vertical force, $m$ is the vendor body mass and $g = 9.81$\,m\,s$^{-2}$. The starting value assumes equal take-off and touch-down speeds. The integral was evaluated as a cumulative sum over samples. The main landing phase is $[t_{\mathrm{td}}, t_{\mathrm{lc}}]$. In the sensitivity analysis, the landing phase is a fixed window from $t_{\mathrm{td}}$ to $t_{\mathrm{td}} + 1$\,s, which is where the vendor's trial record ends.

Five exclusion rules were applied in sequence (Table~\ref{tab:qc}). R1 removed trials whose vendor body mass was missing or not positive, since all curves are divided by body weight. R2 compared that mass with the mass implied by mean force during quiet standing, from the start of the record to 30\,ms before movement onset. Trials that differed by more than 5\% were removed, because they point to a wrongly entered mass or an athlete who was moving. R3 removed trials whose mean force between 20\,ms after take-off and 20\,ms before touch-down exceeded 50\,N in absolute value, which indicates plate contact in flight or a mis-detected event. R4 removed trials with a missing vendor metric or missing age. R5, applied in the main analysis only, removed trials in which $t_{\mathrm{lc}}$ was not reached before the record ended. The rules took the sample from \nTrialsAll{} trials (\nAthAll{} athletes) to \nTrials{} trials (\nAth{} athletes). The 1-s window, which does not need R5, kept \nTrialsS{} trials.

\input{tables/tab1_qc}

Force was divided by body weight, $\tilde F(t) = F(t)/(m g)$. A value of 1 then means quiet standing, and athletes of different mass share one scale. Each phase $[t_a, t_b]$ was resampled by linear interpolation at 101 equally spaced times,
\begin{equation}
\tilde F^{(p)}_k = \tilde F\!\left(t_a + \tfrac{k}{100}\,(t_b - t_a)\right), \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, 100.
\end{equation}
Resampling each phase separately places take-off at the last point of the first block and touch-down at the first point of the second. This is a simple landmark registration \parencite{ramsay2005,richter2014a,moudy2018}. A single time scale for the whole trial would put the same event at different percentages in different trials and mix timing with shape. Flight was left out because force is zero in the air and the only information in that segment is its length, which already defines flight-time jump height \parencite{linthorne2001}. Each trial therefore became a 202-element vector $\mathbf{x}_i$: 101 take-off values followed by 101 landing values.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

PCA was run on the $n \times 202$ matrix of curves \parencite{jolliffe2002,jolliffe2016}. The curves were centred on the mean curve but not scaled, because every element is in the same unit (multiples of body weight). The decomposition is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\top} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top},
\qquad
z_{ik} = \mathbf{v}_k^{\top}(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_k$ is the unit-length loading vector of component $k$, $\lambda_k$ its eigenvalue and $z_{ik}$ the score of trial $i$. Component $k$ explains $\lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_j$ of the total variance. The number of components was set at four before the analysis and was the same for both landing definitions. A fixed number avoids choosing the cut-off after seeing how components relate to the vendor metrics. The scree plot (Fig.~\ref{fig:mean}B) was inspected only to describe the result. Signs were set so that the largest absolute loading of each vector was positive. Three tools aided interpretation. First, curves were reconstructed as $\bar{\mathbf{x}} \pm 2\sqrt{\lambda_k}\,\mathbf{v}_k$ \parencite{brandon2013,warmenhoven2019}. Second, the landing share of each component was computed as $\sum_{j \in \mathcal{L}} v_{jk}^2$, with $\mathcal{L}$ the 101 landing elements. Third, scores were correlated (Pearson) with peak landing force relative to body weight, landing duration $t_{\mathrm{lc}} - t_{\mathrm{td}}$, contraction time, countermovement depth, the four vendor metrics, age and body mass.

The second question was addressed with matched bands. The first band contained trials whose jump height lay between its 45th and 55th percentiles. The second band used the same percentiles of concentric peak force relative to body weight. For each component, the dispersion ratio $\rho_k = \mathrm{SD}(z_{k} \mid i \in \mathcal{B}) / \mathrm{SD}(z_{k})$ divides the score SD inside band $\mathcal{B}$ by the SD in all trials. A ratio near 1 means that matching on the metric leaves curve shape as variable as before. A ratio well below 1 means that the metric restricts curve shape. For the curve as a whole, the ratio was $\rho = \{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{B}})/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S})\}^{1/2}$, with $\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{B}}$ the covariance matrix inside the band. Confidence intervals (CIs, 95\%) for $\rho_k$ were percentile intervals from \nBoot{} bootstrap samples \parencite{efron1993}. The resampling unit was the athlete: each bootstrap sample drew athletes with replacement and kept all their trials. Band limits and PCA loadings were held fixed, so the intervals are conditional on the estimated components.

The third question was addressed by regression. Each score was regressed by ordinary least squares on the four vendor metrics, age and body mass: $z_{ik} = \beta_{0k} + \sum_{q=1}^{6}\beta_{qk}\,w_{iq} + \varepsilon_{ik}$. The coefficient of determination $R^2_k$ is the share of component $k$ that these six variables explain. In-sample $R^2$ overstates fit, and trials from one athlete are correlated. Three checks were therefore added. In five-fold cross-validation, athletes, not trials, were assigned at random to folds \parencite{roberts2017}. Out-of-sample $R^2$ was $1 - \sum_i (z_{ik} - \hat z^{(-f)}_{ik})^2 / \sum_i (z_{ik} - \bar z_k)^2$, where $\hat z^{(-f)}_{ik}$ comes from the model fitted without the fold that holds trial $i$. The model was also refitted on one randomly chosen trial per athlete, and on athlete means of all variables.

To see whether scores describe stable athlete characteristics, each score was split into variance components with a three-level random-intercept model, $z_{ajt} = \mu + a_a + b_{aj} + e_{ajt}$. Here $a_a \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}})$, $b_{aj} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{S}})$ and $e_{ajt} \sim N(0, \sigma^2_{\mathrm{T}})$ are the athlete, session-within-athlete and trial-within-session effects. The model was fitted by restricted maximum likelihood \parencite{pinheiro2000}. The athlete share $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}} / (\sigma^2_{\mathrm{A}} + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{S}} + \sigma^2_{\mathrm{T}})$ is the single-trial intraclass correlation \parencite{hopkins2000,koo2016}. Only \nAthMultiSess{} of the \nAth{} athletes had more than one session, so the session share is imprecise. CIs for all three shares came from \nBoot{} athlete-level bootstrap samples, with athletes relabelled within each sample so that repeated draws counted as separate athletes. The model was refitted with age at test as a fixed effect.

All steps were rerun with the 1-s landing window. The jump-height band was also widened or narrowed (40th--60th percentile; median $\pm$ 1\,cm; median $\pm$ 2\,cm). Results are given as estimates with 95\% CIs and read descriptively. No significance tests were run, so no multiplicity correction applies. One R script (R 4.5.3; \cite{rcore2026}) performed every step, using dplyr 1.2.1, tidyr 1.3.2, ggplot2 4.0.3 \parencite{wickham2016}, patchwork 1.3.2 and nlme 3.1.170 \parencite{pinheiro2000}. PCA used the base function \texttt{prcomp}. All random steps (fold assignment, the one-trial draw and both bootstraps) used seed \seedValue. The script writes every table, figure and number in this paper. A rerun from the de-identified data reproduced all \nReproTotal{} values checked against an earlier, independent run of the same analysis. Reporting followed \textcite{hopkins2009}.

\label{sec:results}

The results follow the order of the three questions. The sample and the modes of variation come first, followed by the matched bands, the regressions, the variance components and the sensitivity analysis.

The 1-s window kept \nTrialsS{} trials, since R5 removed \nLowcomMissing{} trials only from the main analysis. Between \ldsPCD\% and \ldsPCB\% of each retained component's squared loadings fell in the landing block (Table~\ref{tab:pcs}). This concentration follows partly from the method. Unscaled covariance PCA weights each point by its variance, and landing points vary most. PC1 (\varPCA\%) separated two landing types (Fig.~\ref{fig:pcs}). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Taken together, these results answer the question posed at the outset, whether CMJs with similar vendor metrics have similar force--time curves, within the limits of a retrospective design. On a common force scale, the main modes of variation lie in the landing, which held \varShareLanding\% of the point-wise variance. The leading mode describes how quickly the athlete's downward motion is stopped. Matching trials on jump height or relative peak force made their curves only slightly more alike, although the CIs still allow modest narrowing (for PC1 in the jump-height band the lower limit was \jhLoPCA). The four vendor metrics, age and body mass explained at most \rtwoPCC\% of any landing component, and less out of sample. Equal vendor metrics, in short, do not imply equal force--time curves. Nor are the components stable traits of athletes, since most of their variance arose within athletes.

These findings agree with earlier reports that discrete variables give an incomplete account of the jump. \textcite{dowling1993} found no single variable that explained jump performance well. \textcite{richter2014b} and \textcite{floria2019} showed that waveform methods capture performance-related features that discrete points miss. Those studies, like most CMJ waveform work \parencite{cormie2009,sole2018,harry2018}, analysed propulsion and related curve features to performance. We posed the reverse question: how much of the curve do the performance metrics account for? Matching on jump height gives a direct answer, because it holds the outcome nearly constant and shows what variation is left. The 1-s analysis locates that variation. There, a take-off component appeared, the metrics explained about two-thirds of it, and matching on relative peak force narrowed it. This fits the mechanical links among contraction time, peak force, impulse and jump height \parencite{linthorne2001,mcmahon2018}, and the distinction between outcome and execution variables \parencite{harry2024}. The usual vendor metrics thus summarise take-off reasonably well and landing poorly. That is not a flaw in metrics designed to describe propulsion. What routine reports omit is the information in the landing, which every trial records.

PC1 can be read against the landing literature. Injury research has examined fast, high-force landings in young female athletes \parencite{leppanen2017}, and a review found that landing kinetics have some value as injury predictors \parencite{pedley2020}. PC1, which contrasts short, stiff landings with longer, softer ones, resembles this construct. The cited associations, however, come from young female athletes, and their relevance to male footballers is unknown. The link between PC1 and landing duration is mechanical. The main landing ends when downward velocity reaches zero, so the average force over the landing equals body weight plus the touch-down momentum divided by the landing time. A shorter landing must then carry higher forces.

The variance components limit how far the components can be called strategies. The between-athlete share was at most \betweenPCA\%, and no bootstrap upper limit exceeded \baCIMaxUpper\%. By common benchmarks this means poor single-trial reliability \parencite{koo2016}. Averaging helps. For PC1, the mean of \sbTrials{} trials would estimate that session's value with a reliability of about \sbPCA, but characterising the athlete would need several sessions. The landing differences between jumps of equal height therefore mostly reflect how a given trial or session was landed, not a fixed feature of the athlete. This is consistent with work treating movement variability as a normal part of skilled performance \parencite{davids2003,bartlett2007,preatoni2013}, and with the known sensitivity of CMJ variables to trial numbers and protocol \parencite{cormack2008,heishman2020}. Landing also depends on instruction and perceived effort \parencite{hite2025}, neither of which was controlled. Here, \emph{strategy} means the way a given trial was executed. Whether athletes hold stable landing strategies can only be judged from repeated landings under fixed instructions.

Two analytic choices shaped the results. The first is the landing end point. Ending at the lowest COM point confines the landing block to impact absorption and aligns landing timing across trials. A fixed 1-s window fills most of the block with quiet standing. It lowered the variance explained by four components from \cumFour\% to \cumFourS\% and changed what the components described. The second is scaling. Unscaled covariance PCA weights each point by its variance. The take-off block held only \varShareTakeoff\% of the point-wise variance, so take-off contributes little to the leading components unless the landing is short or analysed on its own. Neither choice is wrong, but each answers a different question, and readers should interpret the matched-band and regression results with both in mind.

Because the data came from routine monitoring, they carry limitations that a laboratory study would avoid. Landing technique was not standardised, so some landing variation may reflect instruction, habit or effort rather than physical capacity. The landing end point depends on body mass and on a touch-down velocity inferred from flight time. Both carry error \parencite{street2001}, and the end point was not found in \nLowcomMissing{} trials. All athletes came from one private centre, to which they were referred or came by choice, so the sample does not represent Indian footballers in general. Ages ranged from \ageMin{} to \ageMax{} years, most athletes were adolescents, and maturity status, which affects jumping in young athletes \parencite{malina2015,lloyd2012}, was not recorded. Score dispersion was larger in adults than in athletes under 18 (PC1 SD \sdAdultPCA{} vs \sdMinorPCA), so the pooled results lean towards the adolescent majority. The results may not apply to female athletes or other sports. The regression models were linear, with only four vendor metrics as performance predictors. Adding landing variables would raise the explained share of the landing components. Linear time normalisation combines timing and amplitude differences in one analysis. The fifth and later components, which together held about a quarter of the variance, were left uninterpreted.

Several of these limits point to the next studies. Future work should test whether landing components are stable under repeated, standardised landings, relate them prospectively to injury, fatigue and return to play, include female athletes and other sports, and apply further registration, functional PCA \parencite{ramsay2005,warmenhoven2019} or statistical parametric mapping \parencite{pataky2013} to separate execution variability from consistent technique.

\section{Managerial and practical implications}
\label{sec:implications}

For coaches, sports scientists and the managers who run monitoring programmes, the findings lead to four practical points, each bounded by the fact that most of the variation lay within athletes. First, two jumps of equal height can be landed very differently. A report that ranks or groups athletes by jump height says little about how those jumps were absorbed. Because the differences arose mostly within athletes, they should not be read as fixed athlete characteristics. Second, every CMJ already records the landing at no extra testing cost. Two simple descriptors, landing duration to the lowest COM point and peak landing force relative to body weight, together explained \landRtwoPCA\% of PC1 but only \landRtwoOtherRange\% of PC2 to PC4. Both can be computed from the recorded signal without extra testing, which makes them candidates for routine reports. Third, landing shape varied substantially from trial to trial. Decisions should use the mean of several trials, or of repeated sessions, rather than one landing. Fourth, an unusual landing curve should prompt a closer look at how the athlete lands under fixed instructions. It is not a diagnosis, because no link to injury has been shown in footballers.

A broader point concerns any organisation that relies on instrumented measurement. Commercial software decides which summary numbers users see, and in these data those numbers described take-off well and landing poorly. A standard method applied to data already on file showed variation that the reports did not. Managers who buy measurement systems may benefit from examining the full recorded signal before fixing the set of indicators they report. Extra variation helps decisions only if it repeats across trials, and here much of it did not.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We began by asking whether jumps that look alike in a force-plate report were performed alike. In this sample of male football players they were not: CMJs matched closely on jump height, or on relative peak force, differed in curve shape almost as much as unmatched jumps. The main differences lay in the landing, in particular in how quickly downward motion was stopped. Four vendor metrics, age and body mass explained little of this variation. Most of it arose within athletes, between sessions and between trials, so it should not be read as a stable individual strategy. Every CMJ trial already contains a landing, and it carries information that take-off metrics do not. Reports seldom use it. It deserves a trial in routine force-plate reporting, provided it is averaged over repeated trials and shown to be reproducible under standardised landing instructions.
